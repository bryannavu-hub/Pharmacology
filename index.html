Drug development and approval

A genuine sequence — each stage answers a different question, and nothing skips ahead.

StagePhaseQuestion it answersSubjects
01PreclinicalTwo purposes: (a) does it have the presumed effect in living tissue, and (b) does it cause adverse effects? Tested in vitro (outside a living organism) and in vivo (inside or on one).No humans. Biologic differences mean computer models alone are inadequate.
02Phase IIs it safe? Dose range and toxicity.Small group of healthy volunteers
03Phase IIDoes it work? Effectiveness in the target condition.Patients who have the disease
04Phase IIIIs it better? Compared against existing treatment or placebo.Large, diverse patient population
05FDA approvalMarketing is authorized for specific indications.—
06Phase IVPost-marketing surveillance. Rare and long-term effects that trials were too small or too short to catch.The whole treated public

Phase IV is why drugs get withdrawn after approval. A trial of 3,000 people cannot detect a reaction that occurs in 1 of 50,000 — nurse reporting is how those signals surface.

YearThe disasterThe law it produced
1930s“Elixir of sulfanilamide” was distributed in a vehicle of ethylene glycol that had never been tested in humans. Which law wins: the FDA regulates development and sale, but local laws further regulate distribution and administration. In most cases the strictest law prevails — and rules vary from state to state and even within a state, so know the ones where you practice.

The pregnancy categories are being phased out. The FDA required a pregnancy category on every new drug starting in 1979. In 2014 it added guidelines covering the drug’s presence in human milk, and in 2015 it moved from letter categories to risk levels. Newly approved and revised labels carry no letter — instead they state risk for fertility, pregnancy, and lactation. High risk in pregnancy means research showed fetal toxicity; high risk in lactation means the drug is known to enter human milk and could harm the infant; low risk means no problems shown so far. Learn the letters as your lecture presents them, but expect risk levels on a current insert.

Core concepts

The four definitions the rest of the course is built on. Pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetics are the pair most often confused — keep the direction of the arrow straight.

TermWhat it meansMemory hook
PharmacologyThe study of the biological effects of chemicals (drugs) on living systems.The whole field.
PharmacotherapeuticsPharmacology narrowed to therapy — using drugs to prevent, diagnose, treat, or relieve suffering.Pharmacology with a patient in front of it.
PharmacodynamicsWhat the drug does to the body — receptor binding, enzyme inhibition, selective toxicity.Dynamics = Drug’s action on you.
PharmacokineticsWhat the body does to the drug — absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion.Kinetics = movement; the body moves the drug.

Every drug has both therapeutic and adverse effects. There is no drug with only the effect you want — that idea underpins the entire second half of this deck.

Pharmacodynamics — how drugs act on the body

Karch frames this as four ways drugs work, then explains the receptor machinery underneath. The four-way list is the one most likely to appear as a question.

Drugs usually work in one of four waysMeaning
01Replace or substitute for missing chemicals. Insulin and thyroid hormone are the pattern.
02Increase or stimulate cellular activity.
03Depress or slow cellular activity.
04Interfere with the functioning of foreign cells — invading microorganisms or neoplasms — causing cell death. Drugs acting this way are chemotherapeutic agents.
Receptor site typeHow binding produces an effect
Gated ion channelBinding changes the opening of an ion channel, altering the flow of components into and out of the cell.
Transmembranous receptorSpans the entire cell membrane. The chemical interacts with the receptor outside the cell and affects the portion inside it.
G protein–coupled receptorStimulation makes the receptor contact the G protein, which activates an enzyme or opens a channel as a second messenger.
Intracellular receptorDrugs able to enter the cell react with receptors inside it.
MechanismWhat the drug doesThink of it as
AgonistBinds the receptor and activates it, producing the same effect as the body’s own chemical.A key that turns the lock.
AntagonistBinds the receptor and blocks it, preventing the natural chemical from acting.A key that fits but won’t turn — and jams the lock.
Partial agonistBinds and activates, but produces a weaker response than a full agonist.A key that turns halfway.
Enzyme inhibitionBlocks an enzyme rather than a receptor, interrupting a chemical pathway.Removing a step from the assembly line.
Selective toxicityAttacks the invading organism or abnormal cell while sparing healthy host cells.The goal of every antibiotic and chemo agent — and never perfect, which is why they have side effects.

Pharmacokinetics — ADME

What the body does to the drug, in order. Each stage has a factor that can go wrong and a patient population where it does.

StageDefinitionWhat affects itClinical consequence
AAbsorption
drug → bloodstream		Route (IV, IM, SC, oral, sublingual, inhalation, topical), blood flow to the site, food in the stomach, drug formulation.IV = 100% bioavailable, no absorption phase. Oral is slowest and most variable.
DDistribution
blood → tissues		Perfusion of the tissue, protein binding, and barriers — the blood–brain barrier and the placenta.Only unbound (free) drug is active. Low albumin → more free drug → toxicity at a normal dose.
MMetabolism
biotransformation		Primarily the liver, via the CYP450 enzyme system. Oral drugs undergo the first-pass effect.Liver impairment → drug accumulates. First-pass is why some oral doses are much larger than IV.
EExcretion
drug → out		Mainly the kidneys; also bile, lungs, sweat, and breast milk.Renal impairment → accumulation and toxicity. Breast milk matters for the nursing mother.

First-pass effect: an orally absorbed drug travels through the portal vein to the liver and is partly metabolized before it ever reaches systemic circulation. Any route that bypasses the portal system — IV, IM, sublingual, rectal, transdermal — escapes it.

RouteAbsorption?First-pass?Dr. Kaur’s teaching point
IVNoNoEnters the bloodstream directly — there is no absorption step at all.
OralYesOften yesAbsorbed from the GI tract; many drugs pass through the liver first.
IM / SubQYesNoAbsorbed from tissue into blood; usually bypasses first-pass.
Sublingual / buccalYesNoAbsorbed through oral mucosa; bypasses first-pass.
RectalYesPartial / variableSome venous drainage bypasses the liver, some does not.
Transdermal / inhaledYesNoAbsorbed through skin or lungs; bypasses first-pass.

She corrected this in Week 2 because it gets confused: absorption and first-pass are related but not the same thing. “No first-pass” does not mean “no absorption.” IV is the one route with neither. Topical medications are usually intended for a local effect, though some systemic absorption can occur.

Time course and dosing terms

TermDefinitionWhy the nurse cares
OnsetTime from administration until the drug begins to produce an effect.Tells you when to start looking for a response.
PeakTime at which the drug reaches its highest concentration.When the effect — and the risk of adverse effects — is greatest. Peak levels are drawn here.
DurationHow long the effect lasts.Determines when the next dose is due.
Half-life (t½)Time for the serum concentration to fall by half.Guides dosing frequency. Long half-life → less frequent dosing, greater accumulation risk.
BioavailabilityThe fraction of an administered dose that reaches systemic circulation unchanged.IV is 100% by definition; oral is always less.
TroughThe lowest concentration, drawn just before the next dose.Paired with peak for drugs needing serum monitoring.
Critical concentrationThe amount of drug that must be present at the site of action to produce the desired therapeutic effect.Below it, nothing therapeutic happens; well above it, toxicity. A named learning objective in Karch’s Chapter 2.
Dynamic equilibriumThe actual concentration the drug reaches in the body — the net result of absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion happening at once.Explains why the dose given is not the concentration achieved. Also a named objective.
Active transportMovement of a substance across a cell membrane that requires energy.Contrast with passive diffusion, which does not.

FactorHow it changes the response
WeightStandard doses assume an average adult. Higher body mass may need more; lower needs less. Many drugs are dosed per kg.
AgeThe two extremes are the risk. Infants have immature liver and kidneys; older adults have declining function and altered body composition.
GenderDifferences in body-fat percentage and muscle vascularity affect distribution and absorption — the IM scenario in the deck.

Pathological factorsLiver and kidney disease are the two that change everything — they impair metabolism and excretion.

PsychologicalExpectation and belief affect outcome — the placebo effect is real and measurable.
EnvironmentTemperature, noise, and surroundings alter response, especially for sedatives and analgesics.

Ch 03 · Slides 18–19

Adverse effects and types of reaction

An adverse effect is any undesired drug effect. Reactions are either predictable (extensions of known pharmacology) or unpredictable.

TypeWhat happensPredictable?Example
Primary actionThe intended effect, but too strong — an overdose of the therapeutic action itself.PredictableAn antihypertensive causing hypotension.
Secondary actionAn effect the drug produces in addition to the intended one — ordinary side effects.PredictableAn antihistamine causing drowsiness.
Drug allergyImmune-mediated hypersensitivity. Requires prior sensitizing exposure — not dose-related.UnpredictableRash or anaphylaxis on a second course of penicillin.
IdiosyncraticA strange, unexpected reaction not explained by the drug’s known pharmacology; often genetic.UnpredictableExcitation from a drug that normally sedates.

Why adverse effects happen at all: drugs act on multiple body systems at once, patients differ in sensitivity, dosing may be wrong for the individual, and individual variation is unavoidable.

Organ and tissue toxicity

Grouped by system, with what you assess and the lab that tracks it — this is the table most worth knowing cold. Chapter 3’s objectives are the clinical significance of adverse reactions, the four types of allergic response, drug-induced tissue damage, and applying clinical judgment to them.

SystemToxic effectsWhat to monitor

#RightWhat you verify

Her exact caution: the rights help catch errors — they do not replace assessment, clarification, or stopping when something does not make sense. Her warm-up case makes the point: an order for metoprolol 25 mg with a hold parameter of systolic < 100, and a BP of 88/54. The dose is “right.” You still hold it.

Routes of administration

Route determines how fast the drug arrives and whether it meets the liver first. Onset times are typical ranges, not guarantees.

Pick one and stay with it

The one setup: Desired ÷ Have × Quantity

Karch names three acceptable methods. Dr. Kaur models one, and explicitly lists “memorising three different calculation methods” as de-emphasized. Use hers. The other two are shown once below so you recognise them if a question is phrased that way.

Dose to give = D ÷ H × Q
D = desired / ordered dose  ·  H = dose on hand  ·  Q = quantity containing H

Keep the units visible as you work. They should cancel, or lead you to the unit the question asked for.

Order: 375 mg PO. On hand: 250 mg per 5 mL. How many mL?
  1. Formula (desired over have). (375 ÷ 250) × 5 = 7.5 → 7.5 mL
  2. Ratio–proportion. 250 mg : 5 mL :: 375 mg : x → 250x = 1875 → 7.5 mL
  3. Dimensional analysis. 375 mg × (5 mL / 250 mg) = 7.5 mL. Units cancel, leaving mL → 7.5 mL
Karch’s own example (p. 66). An order is written for 324 mg of aspirin by mouth. The tablets available are 81 mg. How many tablets should the nurse give?
  1. Ratio and proportion, set up as Karch does: amount available / one tablet = amount prescribed / number of tablets
  2. 81 mg / 1 tablet = 324 mg / X → 81X = 324
  3. Answer: 4 tablets

Why dimensional analysis is worth learning: it carries the units through the arithmetic, so if your units do not cancel to the unit you were asked for, you know you set it up wrong before you get a number.

Dr. Kaur’s worked examples

The four she modelled in class, in her setup and with her reasonableness checks.

Tablets. Order: acetaminophen 650 mg PO. Available: 325 mg per tablet.
  1. D = 650 mg, H = 325 mg, Q = 1 tablet. Units already match.
  2. 650 ÷ 325 × 1 → 2 tablets
  3. Reasonableness: 650 mg is twice 325 mg, so 2 tablets makes sense.
Oral liquid. Order: amoxicillin 600 mg PO. Available: 400 mg / 5 mL.
  1. Why Q = 5 mL: the 400 mg on hand is contained in 5 mL, so Q is the 5 mL — not 1.
  2. 600 ÷ 400 × 5 mL → 7.5 mL
  3. Safety habit: measure with an appropriately marked oral dosing device in mL. Never a household spoon.
Weight-based, two steps. Order: cefazolin 25 mg/kg/dose. Patient weighs 20 kg. Prepared concentration 100 mg/mL.
  1. Step 1 — find the ordered dose. 25 mg/kg × 20 kg = 500 mg per dose
  2. Step 2 — convert to what is available. 500 ÷ 100 × 1 mL → 5 mL
  3. Never skip the dose-range or maximum-dose check when the order or policy requires it.
Infusion pump. Infuse 1,000 mL over 8 hours. What rate will you program?
  1. mL/hr = total volume (mL) ÷ time (hr) — hours in the denominator.
  2. 1,000 ÷ 8 → 125 mL/hr
  3. The pump is a tool. Verify the programmed rate against the order before starting the infusion.
Critical-care drip — the format your course uses. Order: propofol 12 mcg/kg/min IV. Weight: 154 lbs. Dose on hand: 500 mg propofol in 50 mL NS. How many mL/hr does the nurse set the pump to? Round to the nearest whole number.
  1. Convert the weight. 154 lb ÷ 2.2 = 70 kg
  2. Put the bag in mcg. 500 mg × 1000 = 500,000 mcg
  3. Concentration. 500,000 mcg ÷ 50 mL = 10,000 mcg/mL
  4. Dose per minute. 12 × 70 = 840 mcg/min
  5. Dose per hour. 840 × 60 = 50,400 mcg/hr
  6. Rate. 50,400 ÷ 10,000 = 5.04 → 5 mL/hr
BSA dosing — chemotherapy orders. Order: cisplatin 65 mg/m². BSA: 1.97 m². How many mg does the nurse administer? Round to the nearest whole number.
  1. BSA dosing is a single multiplication — no conversion, no concentration. dose per m² × BSA
  2. 65 mg/m² × 1.97 m² = 128.05 mg
  3. Round. 128 mg
  4. Sanity check. BSA is near 2, so the answer should be close to double 65 — and 128 is.
Paediatric weight-based dose to a volume. Order: furosemide 2 mg/kg. Weight: 23 lbs. Dose on hand: 8 mg/mL suspension. How many mL? Round the lb conversion to the nearest tenth, and the answer to the nearest tenth.
  1. Convert the weight, and round it now. 23 ÷ 2.2 = 10.4545… → 10.5 kg
  2. Ordered dose. 2 mg/kg × 10.5 kg = 21 mg
  3. Convert to volume. 21 mg ÷ 8 mg/mL = 2.625 mL
  4. Round. 2.6 mL
  5. The instruction that matters. This question says to round the kilogram conversion first, which overrides the usual “do not round early” rule. Here it happens not to change the answer — unrounded gives 2.6136, still 2.6 — but on other numbers it will. Follow whichever rounding the question states.

    Challenges to safe and effective drug therapy

    This chapter is not about lifespan dosing, which is what its title suggests. Karch's five objectives are media and advertising, OTC drugs, herbal and alternative therapies, emergency preparedness, and healthcare costs.

    ChallengeWhat Karch saysNursing implication
    Information overloadPatients arrive influenced by advertising, the internet, and the alternative-therapy industry. The era of the provider being seen as always right is over — patients come with requests and demands.Expect to be asked about specific drugs by name. Correcting bad information is part of the job.
    Media influenceDirect-to-consumer prescription advertising became legal in the 1990s. If an ad states what a drug treats, it must also state contraindications, adverse effects, and precautions — so many ads simply never state the indication, freeing them to use pure imagery.When a patient requests an advertised drug, find out what they think it does.
    OTC drugsMany were “grandfathered in” before modern testing standards, so they were never evaluated to the standard new drugs face. Ipecac is the cautionary case — a standard for years, until the FDA announced in 2003 it was not effective for its intended use.OTC drugs can mask the signs and symptoms of an underlying problem, delaying real diagnosis. Duplicate ingredients across cold and allergy products cause accidental overdose.
    Self-careSelf-diagnosing and determining one’s own treatment needs. The FDA weighs it when considering OTC status — lovastatin was rejected for OTC in 2009 and 2010 because high lipids have no symptoms and need a blood test.Ask specifically about products for headache, colds, and constipation. Patients do not consider OTC drugs “real” drugs and omit them from drug histories.
    Herbal & alternative therapiesThe FDA treats them as dietary supplements, not medications, so they are not tightly regulated. Also includes nondrug measures — imagery, massage, acupuncture, relaxation.Not benign: unexpected effects, toxic reactions, interactions with prescription and OTC drugs, and unknown ingredients that alter potency. The clinician’s job is to balance what the patient wants to use against the prescribed regimen.
    Emergency preparednessSince 9/11, the CDC and Office of Homeland Security address exposure to biologic and chemical weapons. Biologic weapons — “germ warfare” — are bacteria, viruses, or parasites used at scale to incapacitate or destroy a population. The 2001 anthrax mailings and the COVID-19 pandemic are the reference events.Karch directs nurses to cdc.gov and its A–Z index for evolving guidance.
    The opioid crisisOver 75% of drug overdose deaths in 2022 involved an opioid. The CDC reports 224 deaths per day from opioid overdose in 2022, and two-thirds were caused by synthetic opioids — often illicitly manufactured fentanyl.Tighter prescribing has lowered prescription-opioid deaths while illicit-opioid deaths rose. Responses: reversal agents in the hands of first responders and the public, safer delivery research, better chronic pain treatment, more rehabilitation access.
    Illicit & recreational drugsNonprescription drugs with no known therapeutic use, taken to enhance mood or increase pleasure. Alcohol and nicotine are legal drugs that cause serious problems. Marijuana is legal in some states but not sanctioned federally; heroin is illegal at both levels.They interact with prescribed drugs and alter the patient’s response. They also mask underlying medical problems. Karch points to nida.nih.gov.
    Healthcare costsCosts have skyrocketed. Patients are discharged far earlier, and many procedures that once required days of hospitalization no longer involve admission at all.Less monitoring means more responsibility falls on the patient and family — which makes teaching critical, and the nurse is usually the one who does it.
    Insurance coveragePrivate: employment-based, direct-purchased, TRICARE. Public: Medicare (federal), Medicaid (state), Veterans Affairs. Plan types include EPO, HMO, POS, and PPO.High Deductible Health Plans trade a lower premium for higher per-service cost, often paired with an HSA. Tier systems and generic-only coverage change what a patient can actually afford — and some patients have no coverage at all.

    Karch’s own Key Points for this chapter: OTC drugs are deemed safe by the FDA when used as directed and need no prescription; they can mask disease, interact with prescriptions, and be taken above the recommended dose to the point of toxicity. Herbal and alternative therapies are dietary supplements and are not tightly regulated; they can produce unexpected effects and toxic reactions, interact with prescription drugs, and contain unknown ingredients that alter effectiveness and toxicity.

    Don’t confuse the messenger with the receptor. ACh and norepinephrine are the messages; receptors are the receivers. An agonist mimics or activates; an antagonist blocks.

    From the textbook — detail the lecture skippedWhy it matters
    SNS = thoracolumbarIts CNS cells originate in the thoracic and lumbar spinal cord. Short preganglionic axon, ganglia beside the spinal cord, long postganglionic axon.
    PNS = craniosacralIts neurons originate in the cranium and sacral cord. Long preganglionic axon, ganglia near or within the target organ, short postganglionic axon. The vagus is the 10th cranial nerve.
    The sweat gland exceptionSympathetic postganglionic fibres to the sweat glands release ACh, not norepinephrine — so they are sympathetic but cholinergic. A favourite exam trap.
    The adrenal medullaIt never develops postganglionic axons. Its chromaffin cells secrete epinephrine and norepinephrine straight into the bloodstream — which is why epinephrine acts as a hormone as well as a neurotransmitter.
    SphinctersPNS stimulation relaxes the GI and urinary sphincters while the bladder wall contracts — the two together are what allow voiding.
    “Feed and breed”Karch’s alternative name for rest-and-digest, alongside “rest-and-digest.”
    Both preganglionic neurons use AChIn both branches the preganglionic neurotransmitter is acetylcholine. The branches differ at the postganglionic step.

    Irreversible cholinesterase inhibitors are not therapeutic drugs. Karch covers them because they are nerve agents: absorbed by inhalation or through skin, they bind acetylcholinesterase permanently. ACh floods the system — the heart slows and becomes ineffective, pupils and bronchi constrict, secretions pour, and sustained muscle contraction immobilises the diaphragm so breathing stops. The one exception in clinical use is echothiophate iodide, applied topically to the eye.

    Beyond SLUDGE, cholinergic toxicity also brings bradycardia, hypotension, bronchospasm with secretions, and severe weakness. Higher-risk patients: asthma, bradycardia, peptic ulcer disease, and GI or GU obstruction.

    Self-test

    Both questions from the lecture, plus four drawn from the material above. Turn on quiz mode and answer before revealing.

    QuestionAnswer
    An IM injection is given to a 35-year-old male (165 lb) and an 18-year-old female (125 lb). Who absorbs the drug faster, and why?The male. He has more vascular muscle mass, and absorption from an IM site depends on blood flow to that muscle. The female has a higher body-fat percentage and less vascular muscle, so absorption is slower. (Lecture slides 15–16.)
    True or false: poisoning occurs when too much of a drug causes damage to multiple body systems.True. An overdose produces multi-system damage and requires urgent supportive care plus antidote therapy where one exists. (Lecture slides 24–25.)
    A patient with low serum albumin is given a normal dose of a highly protein-bound drug. What happens?More drug remains unbound (free) and pharmacologically active, so a normal dose can produce toxicity. Only free drug exerts an effect.
    Why can the oral dose of a drug be much larger than its IV dose?The first-pass effect. Orally absorbed drug passes through the liver via the portal vein and is partly metabolized before reaching systemic circulation. IV bypasses this entirely.
    A patient develops a rash on the first-ever dose of a drug. Is this most likely an allergy?Unlikely as a true allergy — hypersensitivity requires prior sensitizing exposure. Consider an idiosyncratic or secondary reaction. Still stop the drug, notify, and document.
    Which two organ systems, when diseased, most change how a drug behaves — and which ADME stage does each affect?Liver (metabolism) and kidneys (excretion). Impairment of either causes the drug to accumulate toward toxic levels at ordinary doses.
    Week 2 warm-up. Order: metoprolol 25 mg PO now. MAR: hold if systolic BP < 100. Current BP 88/54. Give it, hold and assess, or ask the patient?Hold and assess / clarify. The dose is correct, but a hold parameter is met. The “right dose” is not enough — assessment and clinical judgment come first. Asking the patient what they prefer does not resolve a safety parameter.
    Mini-case A. Patient says penicillin caused hives. New order: amoxicillin.Stop and clarify before giving. Hives is a true allergic reaction, and amoxicillin is a penicillin-class antibiotic — cross-reactivity is the concern. Verify the reaction, notify the prescriber.
    Mini-case B. Order: acetaminophen 650 mg. Available: 325 mg tablets.Proceed — this is a normal calculation. 650 ÷ 325 × 1 = 2 tablets. Two tablets is a reasonable answer for a dose twice the tablet strength.
    Mini-case C. Order to give now. Patient asks “what is this for?”Stop and teach before giving. Answer the question — name and purpose. An informed patient is the last safety check, and consent depends on it.
    Mini-case D. Order reads 0.5 mg. The MAR displays .5 mg.Clarify. A naked decimal is an ISMP error-prone designation — .5 mg can be read as 5 mg. Confirm the intended dose and report the display as a hazardous condition.
    Integrated case. Ms. Alvarez, 67, discharged yesterday, says she takes half of some pills when money is tight, uses an unlisted OTC sleep aid and herbal supplement, saw a post claiming supplements are safer than prescriptions, and asks what her new pain medicine is for. Order: acetaminophen 650 mg; available 325 mg tablets.Assess: full medication history including the unlisted OTC and herbal, last dose, allergies, pain, liver history (acetaminophen), and her understanding.
    Barriers: cost-driven dose-splitting, polypharmacy with undisclosed products, information quality from social media, a care transition, and a health-literacy gap.
    Teach: name and purpose, the acetaminophen ceiling and its presence in combination OTC products, why the supplement must be disclosed, and cost options — then teach-back.
    Math: 650 ÷ 325 × 1 = 2 tablets, if it is otherwise safe to give.
    Her four layers of a safe medication plan.1. Right patient and assessment — is this appropriate now? 2. Right medication plan — order, dose, route, timing, indication, monitoring. 3. Right calculation — units match, correct math, reasonable answer. 4. Right real-world plan — can the patient understand, obtain, and safely continue it?
