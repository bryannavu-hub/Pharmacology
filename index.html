Drug development and approval

A genuine sequence — each stage answers a different question, and nothing skips ahead.

StagePhaseQuestion it answersSubjects
01PreclinicalTwo purposes: (a) does it have the presumed effect in living tissue, and (b) does it cause adverse effects? Tested in vitro (outside a living organism) and in vivo (inside or on one).No humans. Biologic differences mean computer models alone are inadequate.
02Phase IIs it safe? Dose range and toxicity.Small group of healthy volunteers
03Phase IIDoes it work? Effectiveness in the target condition.Patients who have the disease
04Phase IIIIs it better? Compared against existing treatment or placebo.Large, diverse patient population
05FDA approvalMarketing is authorized for specific indications.—
06Phase IVPost-marketing surveillance. Rare and long-term effects that trials were too small or too short to catch.The whole treated public

Phase IV is why drugs get withdrawn after approval. A trial of 3,000 people cannot detect a reaction that occurs in 1 of 50,000 — nurse reporting is how those signals surface.

YearThe disasterThe law it produced
1930s“Elixir of sulfanilamide” was distributed in a vehicle of ethylene glycol that had never been tested in humans. Ethylene glycol is toxic; hundreds died and many more became very ill.Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938 — gave the FDA power to enforce standards for testing drug toxicity and monitoring labeling.

Which law wins: the FDA regulates development and sale, but local laws further regulate distribution and administration. In most cases the strictest law prevails — and rules vary from state to state and even within a state, so know the ones where you practice.

The pregnancy categories are being phased out. The FDA required a pregnancy category on every new drug starting in 1979. In 2014 it added guidelines covering the drug’s presence in human milk, and in 2015 it moved from letter categories to risk levels. Newly approved and revised labels carry no letter — instead they state risk for fertility, pregnancy, and lactation. High risk in pregnancy means research showed fetal toxicity; high risk in lactation means the drug is known to enter human milk and could harm the infant; low risk means no problems shown so far. Learn the letters as your lecture presents them, but expect risk levels on a current insert.

Core concepts

The four definitions the rest of the course is built on. Pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetics are the pair most often confused — keep the direction of the arrow straight.

TermWhat it meansMemory hook
PharmacologyThe study of the biological effects of chemicals (drugs) on living systems.The whole field.
PharmacotherapeuticsPharmacology narrowed to therapy — using drugs to prevent, diagnose, treat, or relieve suffering.Pharmacology with a patient in front of it.
PharmacodynamicsWhat the drug does to the body — receptor binding, enzyme inhibition, selective toxicity.Dynamics = Drug’s action on you.
PharmacokineticsWhat the body does to the drug — absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion.Kinetics = movement; the body moves the drug.

Every drug has both therapeutic and adverse effects. There is no drug with only the effect you want — that idea underpins the entire second half of this deck.

Pharmacodynamics — how drugs act on the body

Karch frames this as four ways drugs work, then explains the receptor machinery underneath. The four-way list is the one most likely to appear as a question.

Drugs usually work in one of four waysMeaning
01Replace or substitute for missing chemicals. Insulin and thyroid hormone are the pattern.
02Increase or stimulate cellular activity.
03Depress or slow cellular activity.
04Interfere with the functioning of foreign cells — invading microorganisms or neoplasms — causing cell death. Drugs acting this way are chemotherapeutic agents.
Receptor site typeHow binding produces an effect
Gated ion channelBinding changes the opening of an ion channel, altering the flow of components into and out of the cell.
Transmembranous receptorSpans the entire cell membrane. The chemical interacts with the receptor outside the cell and affects the portion inside it.
G protein–coupled receptorStimulation makes the receptor contact the G protein, which activates an enzyme or opens a channel as a second messenger.
Intracellular receptorDrugs able to enter the cell react with receptors inside it.
MechanismWhat the drug doesThink of it as
AgonistBinds the receptor and activates it, producing the same effect as the body’s own chemical.A key that turns the lock.
AntagonistBinds the receptor and blocks it, preventing the natural chemical from acting.A key that fits but won’t turn — and jams the lock.
Partial agonistBinds and activates, but produces a weaker response than a full agonist.A key that turns halfway.
Enzyme inhibitionBlocks an enzyme rather than a receptor, interrupting a chemical pathway.Removing a step from the assembly line.
Selective toxicityAttacks the invading organism or abnormal cell while sparing healthy host cells.The goal of every antibiotic and chemo agent — and never perfect, which is why they have side effects.

Pharmacokinetics — ADME

What the body does to the drug, in order. Each stage has a factor that can go wrong and a patient population where it does.

StageDefinitionWhat affects itClinical consequence
AAbsorption
drug → bloodstream		Route (IV, IM, SC, oral, sublingual, inhalation, topical), blood flow to the site, food in the stomach, drug formulation.IV = 100% bioavailable, no absorption phase. Oral is slowest and most variable.
DDistribution
blood → tissues		Perfusion of the tissue, protein binding, and barriers — the blood–brain barrier and the placenta.Only unbound (free) drug is active. Low albumin → more free drug → toxicity at a normal dose.
MMetabolism
biotransformation		Primarily the liver, via the CYP450 enzyme system. Oral drugs undergo the first-pass effect.Liver impairment → drug accumulates. First-pass is why some oral doses are much larger than IV.
EExcretion
drug → out		Mainly the kidneys; also bile, lungs, sweat, and breast milk.Renal impairment → accumulation and toxicity. Breast milk matters for the nursing mother.

First-pass effect: an orally absorbed drug travels through the portal vein to the liver and is partly metabolized before it ever reaches systemic circulation. Any route that bypasses the portal system — IV, IM, sublingual, rectal, transdermal — escapes it.

RouteAbsorption?First-pass?Dr. Kaur’s teaching point
IVNoNoEnters the bloodstream directly — there is no absorption step at all.
OralYesOften yesAbsorbed from the GI tract; many drugs pass through the liver first.
IM / SubQYesNoAbsorbed from tissue into blood; usually bypasses first-pass.
Sublingual / buccalYesNoAbsorbed through oral mucosa; bypasses first-pass.
RectalYesPartial / variableSome venous drainage bypasses the liver, some does not.
Transdermal / inhaledYesNoAbsorbed through skin or lungs; bypasses first-pass.

She corrected this in Week 2 because it gets confused: absorption and first-pass are related but not the same thing. “No first-pass” does not mean “no absorption.” IV is the one route with neither. Topical medications are usually intended for a local effect, though some systemic absorption can occur.

Time course and dosing terms

TermDefinitionWhy the nurse cares
OnsetTime from administration until the drug begins to produce an effect.Tells you when to start looking for a response.
PeakTime at which the drug reaches its highest concentration.When the effect — and the risk of adverse effects — is greatest. Peak levels are drawn here.
DurationHow long the effect lasts.Determines when the next dose is due.
Half-life (t½)Time for the serum concentration to fall by half.Guides dosing frequency. Long half-life → less frequent dosing, greater accumulation risk.
BioavailabilityThe fraction of an administered dose that reaches systemic circulation unchanged.IV is 100% by definition; oral is always less.
TroughThe lowest concentration, drawn just before the next dose.Paired with peak for drugs needing serum monitoring.
Critical concentrationThe amount of drug that must be present at the site of action to produce the desired therapeutic effect.Below it, nothing therapeutic happens; well above it, toxicity. A named learning objective in Karch’s Chapter 2.
Dynamic equilibriumThe actual concentration the drug reaches in the body — the net result of absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion happening at once.Explains why the dose given is not the concentration achieved. Also a named objective.
Active transportMovement of a substance across a cell membrane that requires energy.Contrast with passive diffusion, which does not.

Factors influencing drug response

Why the same dose of the same drug produces different results in two patients.

FactorHow it changes the response
WeightStandard doses assume an average adult. Higher body mass may need more; lower needs less. Many drugs are dosed per kg.
AgeThe two extremes are the risk. Infants have immature liver and kidneys; older adults have declining function and altered body composition.
GenderDifferences in body-fat percentage and muscle vascularity affect distribution and absorption — the IM scenario in the deck.
Physiological factorsTime of day, hydration, electrolytes, and normal variation.
Pathological factorsLiver and kidney disease are the two that change everything — they impair metabolism and excretion.
GeneticsInherited enzyme variation makes patients fast or slow metabolizers of the same drug.
ImmunologicPrior sensitization sets up allergic response on re-exposure.
PsychologicalExpectation and belief affect outcome — the placebo effect is real and measurable.
EnvironmentTemperature, noise, and surroundings alter response, especially for sedatives and analgesics.
ToleranceRepeated exposure → decreased response → larger doses needed for the same effect.
AccumulationDrug is taken in faster than it is eliminated → concentration builds → toxicity.
Ch 03 · Slides 18–19

Adverse effects and types of reaction

An adverse effect is any undesired drug effect. Reactions are either predictable (extensions of known pharmacology) or unpredictable.

TypeWhat happensPredictable?Example
Primary actionThe intended effect, but too strong — an overdose of the therapeutic action itself.PredictableAn antihypertensive causing hypotension.
Secondary actionAn effect the drug produces in addition to the intended one — ordinary side effects.PredictableAn antihistamine causing drowsiness.
Drug allergyImmune-mediated hypersensitivity. Requires prior sensitizing exposure — not dose-related.UnpredictableRash or anaphylaxis on a second course of penicillin.
IdiosyncraticA strange, unexpected reaction not explained by the drug’s known pharmacology; often genetic.UnpredictableExcitation from a drug that normally sedates.

Why adverse effects happen at all: drugs act on multiple body systems at once, patients differ in sensitivity, dosing may be wrong for the individual, and individual variation is unavoidable.

Organ and tissue toxicity

Grouped by system, with what you assess and the lab that tracks it — this is the table most worth knowing cold. Chapter 3’s objectives are the clinical significance of adverse reactions, the four types of allergic response, drug-induced tissue damage, and applying clinical judgment to them.

SystemToxic effectsWhat to monitor
DermatologicKarch’s range: from a simple rash to potentially fatal exfoliative dermatitis. Also stomatitis (mouth inflammation) and Stevens–Johnson syndrome.Skin and oral mucosa assessment. SJS is a medical emergency — blistering with mucous membrane involvement.
GastrointestinalNausea, vomiting, diarrhea, ulceration and GI bleeding.Intake and output, abdominal assessment, stool for occult blood.

Why the CJMM exists: it was developed by the NCSBN — the body that writes the NCLEX — as a framework for measuring clinical judgment and decision making. That is why Next Gen NCLEX items are phrased in its language rather than the classic five-step vocabulary.

Karch key termDefinition
NursingThe art of nurturing and caring for the sick, combined with the scientific application of chemistry, anatomy, physiology, biology, nutrition, psychology, and pharmacology to the clinical situation.
Nursing processThe problem-solving method used to deliver efficient care: gather information, analyze it into a nursing conclusion, prioritize the concerns, set goals and outcomes, intervene, and evaluate.

The Rights of medication administration

Week 1 taught five. Week 2 expanded it to ten. Dr. Kaur presents them as a safety framework and warns that programs and facilities organise them differently — so use them with clinical judgment, not instead of it.

#RightWhat you verify
01PatientUse two identifiers.
02MedicationMatch the order and MAR against the label.
03DoseCalculate it — then check it for reasonableness.
04RouteCorrect formulation and correct route.
05TimeSchedule, interval, and when the last dose was given.
06ReasonWhy is this patient receiving this drug?
07AssessmentAre the required vitals and labs safe for this dose?
08Education / refusalExplain it; respect questions and the patient’s right to refuse.
09DocumentationDocument what was actually given.

Her exact caution: the rights help catch errors — they do not replace assessment, clarification, or stopping when something does not make sense. Her warm-up case makes the point: an order for metoprolol 25 mg with a hold parameter of systolic < 100, and a BP of 88/54. The dose is “right.” You still hold it.

RightWhat you verifyWhere it goes wrong
Right patientConfirm identity with at least two identifiers — typically name and date of birth.Room number is not an identifier. Patients move.

Many programs add right documentation, right reason, right response, and right to refuse. This deck teaches five — answer five unless the question says otherwise.

Routes of administration

Route determines how fast the drug arrives and whether it meets the liver first. Onset times are typical ranges, not guarantees.

RouteTypical onsetFirst-pass?Key nursing point
Intravenous (IV)30–60 secNoFastest and least reversible — 100% bioavailable. Cleanse the site aseptically and assess the line for patency; do not start if there are signs of vein irritation or infiltration. Check compatibility before running drugs in the same line, and label, date, and time the line.
Intramuscular (IM)10–20 minNoInject deep into muscle at a 90-degree angle. Karch’s recommended sites: ventrogluteal, vastus lateralis, deltoid. Withdraw slowly, engage the safety device, do not recap, dispose in the sharps container.
Subcutaneous (SC)15–30 minNoSlower than IM; small volumes only. Rotate sites with repeated dosing.
Sublingual / buccal3–5 minNo — bypassesAbsorbed straight into systemic circulation. Do not swallow.
Oral (PO)30–90 minYesSafest and most convenient, slowest and most variable. Food and GI status change absorption.
Rectal5–30 minPartialUseful when the patient is vomiting or unconscious. Absorption is erratic.
Inhalationseconds–minNoLarge surface area, rapid uptake. Mostly local respiratory effect.
TransdermalhoursNoSteady sustained levels. Remove the old patch before applying the new one.
TopicalvariesNoLocal effect intended; broken skin increases systemic absorption.

Preventing medication errors: verify the order against the MAR, never give a drug someone else prepared, question any order that is illegible or seems wrong, avoid dangerous abbreviations, and use two identifiers every time. Report errors that reach the patient and near misses — near misses are how systems get fixed.

Pick one and stay with it

The one setup: Desired ÷ Have × Quantity

Karch names three acceptable methods. Dr. Kaur models one, and explicitly lists “memorising three different calculation methods” as de-emphasized. Use hers. The other two are shown once below so you recognise them if a question is phrased that way.

Dose to give = D ÷ H × Q
D = desired / ordered dose  ·  H = dose on hand  ·  Q = quantity containing H

Keep the units visible as you work. They should cancel, or lead you to the unit the question asked for.

Order: 375 mg PO. On hand: 250 mg per 5 mL. How many mL?
  1. Formula (desired over have). (375 ÷ 250) × 5 = 7.5 → 7.5 mL
  2. Ratio–proportion. 250 mg : 5 mL :: 375 mg : x → 250x = 1875 → 7.5 mL
  3. Dimensional analysis. 375 mg × (5 mL / 250 mg) = 7.5 mL. Units cancel, leaving mL → 7.5 mL
Karch’s own example (p. 66). An order is written for 324 mg of aspirin by mouth. The tablets available are 81 mg. How many tablets should the nurse give?
  1. Ratio and proportion, set up as Karch does: amount available / one tablet = amount prescribed / number of tablets
  2. 81 mg / 1 tablet = 324 mg / X → 81X = 324
  3. Answer: 4 tablets

Why dimensional analysis is worth learning: it carries the units through the arithmetic, so if your units do not cancel to the unit you were asked for, you know you set it up wrong before you get a number.

The four she modelled in class, in her setup and with her reasonableness checks.

Tablets. Order: acetaminophen 650 mg PO. Available: 325 mg per tablet.
  1. D = 650 mg, H = 325 mg, Q = 1 tablet. Units already match.
  2. 650 ÷ 325 × 1 → 2 tablets
  3. Reasonableness: 650 mg is twice 325 mg, so 2 tablets makes sense.
Oral liquid. Order: amoxicillin 600 mg PO. Available: 400 mg / 5 mL.
  1. Why Q = 5 mL: the 400 mg on hand is contained in 5 mL, so Q is the 5 mL — not 1.
  2. 600 ÷ 400 × 5 mL → 7.5 mL
  3. Safety habit: measure with an appropriately marked oral dosing device in mL. Never a household spoon.
Weight-based, two steps. Order: cefazolin 25 mg/kg/dose. Patient weighs 20 kg. Prepared concentration 100 mg/mL.
  1. Step 1 — find the ordered dose. 25 mg/kg × 20 kg = 500 mg per dose
  2. Step 2 — convert to what is available. 500 ÷ 100 × 1 mL → 5 mL
  3. Never skip the dose-range or maximum-dose check when the order or policy requires it.
Infusion pump. Infuse 1,000 mL over 8 hours. What rate will you program?
  1. mL/hr = total volume (mL) ÷ time (hr) — hours in the denominator.
  2. 1,000 ÷ 8 → 125 mL/hr
  3. The pump is a tool. Verify the programmed rate against the order before starting the infusion.
Critical-care drip — the format your course uses. Order: propofol 12 mcg/kg/min IV. Weight: 154 lbs. Dose on hand: 500 mg propofol in 50 mL NS. How many mL/hr does the nurse set the pump to? Round to the nearest whole number.
  1. Convert the weight. 154 lb ÷ 2.2 = 70 kg
  2. Put the bag in mcg. 500 mg × 1000 = 500,000 mcg
  3. Concentration. 500,000 mcg ÷ 50 mL = 10,000 mcg/mL
  4. Dose per minute. 12 × 70 = 840 mcg/min
  5. Dose per hour. 840 × 60 = 50,400 mcg/hr
  6. Rate. 50,400 ÷ 10,000 = 5.04 → 5 mL/hr
BSA dosing — chemotherapy orders. Order: cisplatin 65 mg/m². BSA: 1.97 m². How many mg does the nurse administer? Round to the nearest whole number.
  1. BSA dosing is a single multiplication — no conversion, no concentration. dose per m² × BSA
  2. 65 mg/m² × 1.97 m² = 128.05 mg
  3. Round. 128 mg
  4. Sanity check. BSA is near 2, so the answer should be close to double 65 — and 128 is.
Paediatric weight-based dose to a volume. Order: furosemide 2 mg/kg. Weight: 23 lbs. Dose on hand: 8 mg/mL suspension. How many mL? Round the lb conversion to the nearest tenth, and the answer to the nearest tenth.
  1. Convert the weight, and round it now. 23 ÷ 2.2 = 10.4545… → 10.5 kg
  2. Ordered dose. 2 mg/kg × 10.5 kg = 21 mg
  3. Convert to volume. 21 mg ÷ 8 mg/mL = 2.625 mL
  4. Round. 2.6 mL
  5. The instruction that matters. This question says to round the kilogram conversion first, which overrides the usual “do not round early” rule.     Learn this chain once and ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNI and spironolactone all become obvious. Read it as a story: the kidney thinks it is being underperfused, so it calls for more pressure and more volume.

    StepWhat happensConsequence you can see in a patientBlocked by

    Now reverse it — this is the whole safety profile. Block the pathway anywhere and you get the opposite of step 5 and step 7: vasodilation → hypotension, and less aldosterone → less K⁺ excreted → potassium rises. You did not memorise “ACE inhibitors cause hyperkalemia.” You derived it. The same reasoning tells you why spironolactone, which blocks the last step, raises potassium the most.

    And the kidney catch. Angiotensin II constricts the efferent arteriole — the vessel leaving the glomerulus — which is how the kidney maintains filtration pressure when perfusion is poor. Remove angiotensin II and that support disappears, so creatinine can rise when you start an ACEI or ARB. A small rise is expected. A large one means the kidney was depending on that support. This is why renal function is checked before and after starting, not just before.

    ElementDetail
    ActionPositive inotrope — inhibits the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump, so intracellular sodium rises, which drives calcium into the cell → stronger contraction. Separately, it increases vagal tone → slower SA rate and slower AV conduction.

    • Older adults: markedly higher risk of symptomatic hypotension and falls; start at the lower dose and titrate slowly with orthostatic checks.
    • Renal impairment or volume depletion: hypotension and kidney injury more likely — correct volume first and reduce the starting dose.
    • Patients on spironolactone: additive hyperkalemia — a very common heart-failure pairing that needs deliberate potassium monitoring.
    Beta blockers
    “-olol”metoprolol · atenolol · carvedilol
    Example drugsmetoprolol · atenolol · carvedilol
    Mechanism of action
    • Block β1 receptors → HR, contractility and AV conduction all fall
    • Reduced renin release adds a second BP-lowering route
    • Carvedilol also blocks α1 → extra vasodilation
    • Cardioselectivity is dose-dependent and lost at higher doses
    Therapeutic uses
    • Hypertension
    • Angina — lowers myocardial oxygen demand
    • Rate control in atrial fibrillation
    • Post-MI — reduces reinfarction
    • Stable chronic heart failure, titrated slowly
    Side & adverse effects
    • Common: fatigue, dizziness, bradycardia, cold extremities, reduced exercise tolerance
    • Serious: symptomatic bradycardia or heart block, hypotension, heart-failure decompensation if titrated too fast, bronchospasm, rebound ischemia or infarction on abrupt withdrawal, masked hypoglycemia
    Nursing considerations
    1Assessment
    • Apical pulse for one full minute before each dose, against the ordered hold parameters — not a remembered number
    • Respiratory history before the first dose — asthma or COPD changes the agent
    • Dizziness, syncope, falls and activity tolerance
    • New edema, weight gain or dyspnea suggesting decompensation during titration
    • Scan the MAR for diltiazem or verapamil — two AV-nodal blockers together is additive
    2Vitals
    • HR and BP before every dose; hold and notify per the written order
    • Recheck about an hour after an oral dose, at peak effect
    • Lying and standing BP if dizziness is reported
    • Daily weight during heart-failure titration
    3Labs
    • Blood glucose in diabetes — the drug masks hypoglycemic tachycardia
    • Renal function — atenolol is renally cleared and accumulates; metoprolol and carvedilol are hepatic
    • Electrolytes with concurrent diuretics
    • Lipid panel periodically
    4Patient education
    • Never stop suddenly — rebound chest pain, dangerous rhythms or a heart attack can follow
    • Take metoprolol and carvedilol with food
    • Check your pulse at home; report a rate under 60 or new dizziness
    • In diabetes, rely on sweating and hunger to detect a low — the racing heart may be absent
    • Rise slowly; report a 2–3 lb weight gain in a day or new ankle swelling
    • Fatigue in the first weeks is common and usually improves
    Special populations
    • Older adults: increased hypotension, bradycardia, dizziness and falls; start low, check orthostatic BP, review the list for additive hypotensives.
    • Diabetes: masks the tachycardia, tremor and palpitations that warn of hypoglycemia — teach sweating and hunger as the remaining cues and monitor glucose more often at initiation.
    • Asthma and COPD: cardioselective agents preferred, but selectivity is lost at higher doses — monitor for wheeze; carvedilol is nonselective and generally avoided.
    • Acute decompensated heart failure: not the time to start or increase — these are for the stable patient; starting during decompensation worsens it.
    • Renal impairment: atenolol accumulates and needs dose reduction; metoprolol is the safer choice.
    • Pregnancy: labetalol is preferred; atenolol is linked to fetal growth restriction and is avoided.
    Calcium channel blockers — dihydropyridine
    vascularamlodipine · nifedipine · felodipine
    Example drugsamlodipine · nifedipine · felodipine
    Mechanism of action
    • Block calcium entry into vascular smooth muscle
    • Arterioles relax → SVR falls → BP falls
    • Minimal effect on the AV node at usual doses — this is what separates them from diltiazem and verapamil
    Therapeutic uses
    • Hypertension
    • Chronic stable angina — and vasospastic (Prinzmetal) angina
    • Raynaud phenomenon in selected patients
    Side & adverse effects
    • Common: peripheral edema, dizziness, flushing, headache, gingival hyperplasia
    • Serious: symptomatic hypotension; reflex tachycardia, marked with short-acting nifedipine and a reason short-acting forms are avoided in hypertension
    Nursing considerations
    1Assessment
    • Characterise any edema before calling it fluid overload — ask for weight trend, lung sounds and dyspnea; drug-induced edema comes without those
    • Baseline BP and edema assessment for comparison
    • Dizziness, falls risk and activity tolerance
    • Gum assessment at follow-up — gingival overgrowth is a real and reversible effect
    • Ask about grapefruit juice, which raises levels of several agents in this class
    2Vitals
    • BP before dosing and at peak; orthostatic BP during titration
    • HR — a rise suggests reflex tachycardia
    • Ankle and pretibial edema, measured the same way each time
    • Daily weight to distinguish drug edema from fluid overload
    3Labs
    • Renal and hepatic function at baseline
    • Electrolytes if a diuretic is added for the edema
    • No routine drug levels
    4Patient education
    • Ankle swelling is common with this drug and is not the same as fluid overload — report it, but do not panic; elevating the legs and compression help, and your provider may change the dose or drug
    • Call if swelling arrives together with weight gain, breathlessness or waking short of breath — that combination is different and needs assessment
    • Rise slowly; expect flushing and headache early on
    • Avoid grapefruit juice
    • Brush and floss well and keep dental visits — this drug can cause gum overgrowth
    • Do not stop abruptly if you are taking it for angina
    Special populations
    • Older adults: higher risk of orthostatic hypotension, dizziness and falls, and peripheral edema is both more common and more likely to be misread as heart failure; assess orthostatic vitals and always pair edema with a weight trend and lung sounds.
    • Heart failure: dihydropyridines are comparatively safe here (unlike the non-dihydropyridines), but new edema must still be investigated rather than assumed to be the drug.
    • Severe aortic stenosis: vasodilation can drop coronary perfusion dangerously — use with caution.
    • Patients already on a diuretic: a diuretic will not fix dihydropyridine edema, because total body water is normal — adding one risks dehydration and acute kidney injury.
    • Hepatic impairment: these are hepatically metabolised; lower doses are needed.
    • Pregnancy: nifedipine is used for hypertension in pregnancy and for tocolysis; monitor BP closely.
    Calcium channel blockers — non-dihydropyridine
    cardiacdiltiazem · verapamil
    Example drugsdiltiazem · verapamil
    Mechanism of action
    • Block calcium entry in the AV node and myocardium as well as vessels
    • HR falls, AV conduction slows, contractility falls
    • Verapamil is the more cardiac-depressant of the two; diltiazem sits between verapamil and the dihydropyridines
    Therapeutic uses
    • Rate control in atrial fibrillation and flutter
    • Supraventricular tachycardia
    • Angina, including vasospastic
    • Hypertension when rate control is also wanted
    Side & adverse effects
    • Common: bradycardia, dizziness, fatigue, headache; verapamil → constipation, often severe
    • Serious: symptomatic bradycardia, AV block, hypotension, worsening heart failure from negative inotropy
    Nursing considerations
    1Assessment
    • Apical HR and BP before each dose — both, because this class hits rate as well as pressure
    • Check the MAR for a beta blocker — the combination is additive at the AV node and is a genuine cause of heart block
    • Heart-failure history — reduced contractility can decompensate a weak ventricle
    • Bowel pattern before and during verapamil — constipation is the effect that most often drives patients to stop
    • Grapefruit juice use
    2Vitals
    • HR and BP before every dose, held against ordered parameters
    • Cardiac rhythm where monitored — watch for lengthening conduction
    • Orthostatic BP
    • Daily weight and lung sounds if there is any heart-failure history
    3Labs
    • Renal and hepatic function
    • Digoxin level if co-prescribed — verapamil raises digoxin concentrations
    • Electrolytes with concurrent diuretics
    4Patient education
    • Constipation is expected with verapamil and is worth planning for — fluids, fibre and activity from day one, not after it becomes a problem; report no movement for three days
    • Check your pulse; report under 60, or dizziness and fainting
    • Avoid grapefruit juice
    • Do not stop abruptly if taking it for angina or rate control
    • Tell any provider you take this before starting a new heart medicine
    • Rise slowly
    Special populations
    • Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction: generally contraindicated — the negative inotropic effect can decompensate an already failing ventricle. This is the sharpest difference from the dihydropyridines and a favourite exam distinction.
    • Concurrent beta blocker: additive AV-nodal suppression → severe bradycardia and heart block; requires deliberate HR monitoring and often is avoided altogether.
    • Second- or third-degree heart block or sick sinus syndrome without a pacemaker: contraindicated.
    • Older adults: more sensitive to bradycardia and hypotension, and verapamil-induced constipation can progress to impaction or obstruction — assess bowel function at every visit, not just vitals.
    • Patients on digoxin: verapamil raises digoxin levels — anticipate toxicity and confirm a level is being followed.
    • Hepatic impairment: reduced clearance; lower doses required.
    Loop diureticsfurosemide · bumetanide · torsemide
    Example drugsfurosemide · bumetanide · torsemide
    Mechanism of action
    • Block the Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ transporter in the ascending loop of Henle
    • Large amounts of sodium, chloride and water are excreted — the most powerful diuretic class
    • Potassium and magnesium are lost with them
    • The same transporter drives calcium reabsorption, so calcium is lost too
    • Still works when renal function is poor, unlike thiazides
    Therapeutic uses
    • Acute and chronic fluid overload — pulmonary edema, heart failure, ascites
    • Edema from renal or hepatic disease
    • Hypertension when a thiazide is insufficient or renal function is reduced
    • Hypercalcemia — a direct use of the calcium loss
    Side & adverse effects
    • Common: increased urination, orthostatic hypotension, dizziness, thirst, muscle cramps
    • Serious: hypokalemia and resulting dysrhythmias, hyponatremia, hypomagnesemia, dehydration and acute kidney injury, ototoxicity with rapid or high-dose IV, hyperglycemia, hyperuricemia
    Nursing considerations
    1Assessment
    • Weight, using the same scale at the same time after voiding — roughly 1 kg equals 1 litre of fluid, and weight moves before crackles appear
    • Lung sounds, respiratory effort, edema and JVD — the congestion you are treating
    • Strict intake and output; time to and volume of the first void after an IV dose
    • The most recent potassium, and whether digoxin is on the list — these two facts together change what this dose means
    • Hydration and orthostatic symptoms; skin turgor and mucous membranes
    • Hearing and tinnitus with high-dose or repeated IV therapy
    • Sulfonamide allergy history
    2Vitals
    • BP before and after — including orthostatic BP before the first ambulation
    • HR — a rise with a falling BP suggests volume depletion
    • RR, SpO2 and lung sounds — improvement is the therapeutic endpoint in pulmonary congestion
    • Daily weight trend, which is a vital sign in heart failure
    3Labs
    • Potassium — the central lab; hypokalemia drives dysrhythmia and digoxin toxicity
    • Sodium — hyponatremia is common and causes confusion in older adults
    • Magnesium — low magnesium makes hypokalemia refractory and adds dysrhythmia risk
    • BUN and creatinine — over-diuresis causes prerenal injury; a rising BUN:creatinine ratio is the early sign
    • Glucose and uric acid — both rise
    • Digoxin level if co-prescribed
    4Patient education
    • Weigh yourself every morning — same scale, after urinating, before breakfast, similar clothing — and write it down; report a gain of 2–3 lb in a day or 5 lb in a week
    • Take it in the morning, and a second dose no later than late afternoon, so you are not up all night
    • Report muscle cramps, severe weakness, palpitations or a fluttering heartbeat — these can mean your potassium is low
    • Report ringing in the ears or any change in hearing
    • Rise slowly and sit at the bedside before standing
    • Eat potassium-rich foods if your provider advises it — bananas, oranges, potatoes, spinach
    • Report urinating much less than usual
    Special populations
    • Older adults: markedly increased risk of dehydration, orthostatic hypotension, falls and acute kidney injury; hyponatremia frequently presents as new confusion rather than a complaint, so a change in mental status in an older adult on a diuretic is a lab question, not a dementia question.
    • Patients on digoxin: the highest-priority pairing in this unit — diuretic-induced hypokalemia potentiates digoxin at the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and causes toxicity at a normal serum level; potassium must be monitored as part of digoxin safety.
    • Sulfonamide allergy: furosemide, bumetanide and torsemide are sulfonamide derivatives — document the nature of the reaction before the first dose.
    • Renal impairment: higher doses are often needed because the drug must reach the tubular lumen — but the line between effective diuresis and prerenal injury is narrow.
    • Hepatic disease with ascites: over-rapid diuresis precipitates hepatic encephalopathy and hepatorenal syndrome — diuresis is deliberately slower.
    • Concurrent aminoglycosides: additive ototoxicity — a combination worth flagging.
    • Diabetes and gout: glucose and uric acid both rise; anticipate closer glucose monitoring and possible gout flares.
    Thiazide diureticshydrochlorothiazide · chlorthalidone · metolazone
    Example drugshydrochlorothiazide · chlorthalidone · metolazone
    Mechanism of action
    • Block the Na⁺/Cl⁻ symporter in the distal convoluted tubule
    • Moderate sodium and water loss; potassium is lost with it
    • Acting beyond the loop, they increase calcium reabsorption — the opposite of loop diuretics
    • Lose effectiveness as renal function declines
    Therapeutic uses
    • Hypertension — a first-line agent
    • Mild fluid retention
    • Prevention of recurrent calcium kidney stones, using the calcium retention directly
    • Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus
    Side & adverse effects
    • Common: increased urination, dizziness, orthostatic hypotension, photosensitivity, erectile dysfunction
    • Serious: hypokalemia, hyponatremia, hypomagnesemia, hypercalcemia, hyperglycemia, hyperuricemia with gout flare, pancreatitis, rare severe skin reactions
    Nursing considerations
    1Assessment
    • Baseline BP, weight and electrolytes
    • Ask about gout — a flare is a common and avoidable reason patients stop the drug
    • Diabetes status, since glucose control can worsen
    • Current potassium and whether digoxin is on the list — the same interaction as loop diuretics, and easier to overlook because thiazides feel milder
    • Renal function — below roughly 30 mL/min they largely stop working, and continuing one is ineffective therapy
    • Sun exposure habits — photosensitivity is real and underexplained
    2Vitals
    • BP at baseline and follow-up; orthostatic BP during initiation
    • HR for volume-depletion tachycardia
    • Weight trend where fluid management is the goal
    3Labs
    • Potassium — check at baseline and 1–2 weeks after starting or any change
    • Sodium — thiazides cause hyponatremia more often than loops, particularly in older women
    • Calcium — rises; can unmask hyperparathyroidism
    • Glucose, uric acid, magnesium
    • BUN and creatinine
    4Patient education
    • Take it in the morning so increased urination does not disrupt your sleep
    • Use sunscreen and protective clothing — this drug makes your skin burn more easily
    • Report muscle cramps, weakness or palpitations — possible low potassium
    • Report a painful, hot, swollen joint, especially the big toe — it may be gout
    • Rise slowly from sitting or lying
    • It may take 2–4 weeks for the full blood-pressure effect; keep taking it even though you feel no different
    • Keep lab appointments — the blood tests check salts your kidney is losing
    Special populations
    • Older adults, especially older women: the population most prone to thiazide-induced hyponatremia, which presents as confusion, falls or seizures rather than a reported symptom — check sodium at baseline, at 1–2 weeks, and with any mental-status change.
    • Renal impairment: ineffective below roughly 30 mL/min — a patient whose BP is uncontrolled on a thiazide with poor renal function may simply be on the wrong diuretic; metolazone is the exception that retains activity.
    • Gout: raises uric acid and precipitates acute flares — ask about history before starting.
    • Diabetes: raises blood glucose and may need therapy adjustment; monitor more closely after starting.
    • Hypercalcemia or hyperparathyroidism: thiazides retain calcium and can worsen it — conversely this is exactly why they are chosen for calcium stone formers.
    • Patients on digoxin or lithium: hypokalemia potentiates digoxin, and sodium loss raises lithium levels toward toxicity.
    • Sulfonamide allergy: thiazides are sulfonamide derivatives — clarify the reaction history.
    Potassium-sparing diuretics
    aldosterone antagonistsspironolactone · eplerenone · triamterene
    Example drugsspironolactone · eplerenone · triamterene
    Mechanism of action
    • Spironolactone and eplerenone block the aldosterone receptor in the distal tubule and collecting duct
    • Sodium and water are excreted while potassium is retained
    • Triamterene blocks the sodium channel directly, sparing potassium by a different route
    • Weak as diuretics — in heart failure the benefit is blocking aldosterone-driven cardiac remodelling and fibrosis, not fluid removal
    Therapeutic uses
    • Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction — improves survival in selected patients
    • Resistant hypertension
    • Primary hyperaldosteronism
    • Ascites and edema in hepatic cirrhosis
    • Acne, hirsutism and PCOS (spironolactone, via antiandrogen effect)
    Side & adverse effects
    • Common: gynecomastia and breast tenderness, menstrual irregularity, dizziness, GI upset (spironolactone; eplerenone far less)
    • Serious: hyperkalemia — potentially fatal dysrhythmia, dehydration, hyponatremia, acute kidney injury, metabolic acidosis
    Nursing considerations
    1Assessment
    • A current potassium and creatinine before the first dose, and again after starting — this drug is not started on old labs
    • Scan the whole MAR for every other potassium-raising drug: ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNI, potassium supplements, trimethoprim, NSAIDs
    • Ask directly about salt substitutes — potassium chloride, and almost never reported as a medication
    • Baseline renal function — declining kidneys turn a safe drug into a dangerous one
    • Weight, edema and lung sounds for the heart-failure picture
    • Ask about breast tenderness or swelling at follow-up — patients rarely raise it unprompted and it is a common reason for silent non-adherence
    2Vitals
    • BP before dosing and orthostatic BP during titration
    • HR and rhythm — bradycardia and irregularity may be the first sign of hyperkalemia
    • Daily weight and I&O
    • Lung sounds and edema
    3Labs
    • Potassium — the defining lab; baseline, within about a week of starting, after every dose change, and whenever renal function shifts
    • Creatinine and BUN — hyperkalemia risk climbs as renal function falls
    • Sodium
    • Digoxin level if co-prescribed — spironolactone can raise it
    4Patient education
    • Do not use salt substitutes — nearly all of them are potassium, and this drug already holds potassium in
    • Avoid potassium supplements unless specifically prescribed; check with the pharmacist before any new supplement
    • Report muscle weakness, numbness or tingling, a slow or irregular heartbeat, or feeling faint — these can be signs of high potassium
    • Men: breast tenderness or swelling can occur and is reversible — tell your provider rather than stopping the drug on your own
    • Take with food to reduce stomach upset
    • Keep every lab appointment — the potassium check is the reason this drug is safe
    • Weigh daily and report rapid gains
    Special populations
    • Renal impairment: the single greatest risk factor for fatal hyperkalemia on this drug — the kidney is the only meaningful route for potassium excretion, so declining function converts a therapeutic dose into a toxic one; potassium and creatinine must be rechecked whenever renal function changes.
    • On an ACE inhibitor, ARB or ARNI: additive potassium retention, and an extremely common heart-failure combination — treat potassium as a property of the whole regimen, not one drug.
    • Older adults: reduced renal reserve plus polypharmacy plus dehydration risk; check potassium more often and review for NSAIDs and trimethoprim, which add to the risk.
    • Diabetes: hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism already impairs potassium excretion — baseline risk is higher before the drug is even started.
    • Men on spironolactone: gynecomastia is dose-related and a frequent cause of quiet non-adherence — ask about it; eplerenone is the alternative without the antiandrogen effect.
    • Pregnancy: spironolactone's antiandrogen activity raises theoretical concern for a male fetus; eplerenone is generally preferred if one is required.
    Cardiac glycosidesdigoxin
    Example drugssingle-agent class digoxin
    Mechanism of action
    • Inhibits the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump → intracellular sodium rises → the sodium–calcium exchanger leaves more calcium inside the cell
    • More intracellular calcium → stronger contraction (positive inotrope)
    • Separately increases vagal tone → slower SA rate and slower AV conduction (negative chronotrope and dromotrope)
    • Digoxin and potassium compete for the same binding site on that pump — which is why low potassium intensifies the drug's effect without changing its serum level
    Therapeutic uses
    • Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction — symptom relief in selected patients
    • Rate control in atrial fibrillation, usually as an add-on
    Side & adverse effects
    • Common: bradycardia, dizziness, headache
    • Toxicity — GI first: anorexia, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain
    • Toxicity — visual: blurred vision, yellow-green discolouration, halos around lights
    • Toxicity — cardiac, the dangerous one: severe bradycardia, AV block, ventricular ectopy and lethal dysrhythmias
    Nursing considerations
    1Assessment
    • Apical pulse for one full minute before every dose — radial and 30-second counts are not adequate, because the rhythm may be irregular
    • Most recent potassium, and every drug on the list that moves it — furosemide and thiazides are the usual culprits
    • Ask about appetite and nausea: anorexia is typically the earliest toxicity sign and is easily dismissed
    • Ask about vision: blurring, yellow tint, halos
    • Renal function — digoxin is renally cleared and accumulates when kidneys decline
    • Serum digoxin level if ordered, drawn at least 6–8 h after the dose
    • Review for interacting drugs: amiodarone, verapamil, quinidine and clarithromycin all raise digoxin levels
    2Vitals
    • Apical HR — a full minute, every dose, compared against the ordered parameters
    • Rhythm where monitored; new irregularity in a previously regular patient is a warning
    • BP
    • Weight and I&O as heart-failure markers
    3Labs
    • Potassium — equal in importance to the digoxin level itself; hypokalemia causes toxicity at a therapeutic concentration
    • Magnesium (low adds risk) and calcium (high adds risk)
    • Creatinine and BUN — declining renal function is a common route into toxicity in older adults
    • Serum digoxin: heart-failure target often 0.5–0.9 ng/mL; risk rises above 2.0, but a normal level does not exclude toxicity
    4Patient education
    • Take your pulse for a full minute before each dose and record it; hold and call if it is below the number your provider gave you
    • Report loss of appetite, nausea or vomiting — this is usually the first warning, and it is not just an upset stomach
    • Report blurred or yellow-tinted vision, or halos around lights
    • Take it at the same time every day; do not double up on a missed dose
    • Do not start any new medicine, including over-the-counter and herbal products, without checking — several change digoxin levels
    • Keep lab appointments; potassium matters as much as the digoxin level
    • Tell any provider you take digoxin before a new prescription
    Special populations
    • Older adults: the highest-risk group — reduced renal clearance and lower lean body mass raise levels at unchanged doses, and toxicity often presents as confusion, anorexia or falls rather than a classic complaint; lower doses are used and renal function and potassium must be followed.
    • Hypokalemia from any cause: the central interaction — furosemide, thiazides, vomiting or diarrhea all potentiate digoxin at the pump; check potassium before giving and anticipate a fall after any diuretic increase.
    • Renal impairment: digoxin is renally cleared — a rising creatinine means a rising digoxin level even with no dose change.
    • Concurrent amiodarone, verapamil, quinidine or clarithromycin: all raise digoxin concentrations; a dose reduction and level check are usually needed.
    • Hypomagnesemia and hypercalcemia: both increase toxicity risk independently of potassium.
    • Thyroid disease: hypothyroid patients are more sensitive and hyperthyroid patients more resistant — dose requirements change with thyroid status.
    • Severe toxicity: treated with digoxin immune Fab; after it is given, serum digoxin levels become uninterpretable.

    Read it across, not down. The reason this unit is hard is that the classes interact. Open ACE inhibitors, spironolactone and loop diuretics together and read only the labs rows: every one of them says potassium, for opposite reasons. That single lab is the thread running through the whole chapter, and it is where the exam questions live.

    Nonselective adrenergic agonists
    α1 β1 β2epinephrine · norepinephrine · dopamine
    Example drugsepinephrine · norepinephrine · dopamine
    Mechanism of action
    • Stimulate α and β receptors at once
    • α1 → vasoconstriction, BP rises
    • β1 → rate and contractility rise
    • β2 → bronchodilation
    • Also trigger glycogenolysis, raising glucose
    Therapeutic uses
    • Anaphylaxis (epinephrine is first choice)
    • Cardiac arrest
    • Septic, cardiogenic and hypovolemic shock (norepinephrine often preferred)
    • Dopamine when renal perfusion must be preserved
    Side & adverse effects
    • Common: tachycardia, palpitations, tremor, anxiety, headache
    • Serious: hypertensive crisis, dysrhythmias, myocardial ischemia, tissue necrosis from extravasation
    Nursing considerations
    1Assessment
    • Airway, breathing and level of consciousness before and after
    • IV site every 1–2 h for blanching, coolness or swelling — extravasation causes necrosis
    • Peripheral pulses, capillary refill and skin colour for perfusion
    • Urine output as a perfusion marker
    2Vitals
    • Continuous cardiac monitoring; HR and rhythm every 15 min during titration
    • BP continuously by arterial line, or every 5–15 min by cuff
    • RR and SpO2 — confirm the airway actually opened in anaphylaxis
    • Temperature if sepsis is the cause
    3Labs
    • Blood glucose — these raise it via glycogenolysis; check in diabetes
    • Serum lactate to track perfusion in shock
    • BUN and creatinine for renal perfusion
    • Electrolytes, especially potassium, during prolonged infusion
    4Patient education
    • For an EpiPen: carry two at all times, inject into the outer thigh through clothing, then call 911 even if you feel better — symptoms can return hours later
    • Expect a pounding heart and shakiness; that is the drug working, not an allergy
    • Check the expiry date and that the solution is clear
    • Report chest pain or an irregular heartbeat immediately
    Special populations
    • Older adults: higher risk of dysrhythmia and myocardial ischemia because of reduced coronary reserve; monitor ECG continuously and report chest pain at once.
    • Cardiac disease: increased myocardial oxygen demand can precipitate angina or infarction; not a reason to withhold in anaphylaxis, where the risk of not giving it is greater.
    • Diabetes: glycogenolysis raises glucose; check more often and anticipate a temporary insulin adjustment.
    • Pregnancy: epinephrine is used for anaphylaxis — untreated maternal anaphylaxis causes fetal hypoxia.
    • Peripheral vascular disease: vasoconstriction can worsen ischemia; assess extremities each shift.
    Alpha-1 adrenergic agonistsphenylephrine · midodrine
    Example drugsphenylephrine · midodrine
    Mechanism of action
    • Selectively stimulate α1 receptors on vascular smooth muscle
    • Peripheral vasoconstriction raises systemic vascular resistance and BP
    • Little direct effect on heart or bronchi
    Therapeutic uses
    • Hypotension and shock states
    • Maintaining BP during spinal anaesthesia
    • Nasal and ophthalmic decongestion
    • Pupil dilation for eye examination
    Side & adverse effects
    • Common: reflex bradycardia, headache, restlessness
    • Serious: severe hypertension, reduced organ perfusion, extravasation injury
    Nursing considerations
    1Assessment
    • Check BP and volume status before starting — a vasopressor does not correct hypovolemia
    • IV site hourly for infiltration
    • Mental status and urine output as perfusion indicators
    • Ask about home OTC decongestants to avoid duplication
    2Vitals
    • BP every 5–15 min during titration to the ordered MAP goal
    • HR — expect reflex bradycardia as BP rises; this is predictable, not an error
    • Continuous cardiac rhythm
    3Labs
    • BUN and creatinine — excessive vasoconstriction reduces renal perfusion
    • Lactate to confirm perfusion is improving
    • Electrolytes during prolonged infusion
    4Patient education
    • Rise slowly from sitting or lying (midodrine)
    • Take the last midodrine dose at least 4 h before bedtime to avoid supine hypertension
    • Do not use OTC decongestants without asking the pharmacist
    • Report headache, chest pain or a pounding heartbeat
    Special populations
    • Older adults: more sensitive to BP swings and at risk of supine hypertension with midodrine; check BP sitting and lying and keep the head of the bed elevated.
    • Hypertension or vascular disease: may provoke dangerous BP elevation; use the lowest effective dose and monitor continuously.
    • Cardiac disease: reflex bradycardia is poorly tolerated with existing conduction disease — watch for heart block.
    • Note: the FDA has proposed removing oral phenylephrine from OTC products because effectiveness for congestion is disputed.
    Beta-1 adrenergic agonists
    inotropesdobutamine · dopamine · isoproterenol
    Example drugstextbook only dobutamine · dopamine · isoproterenol
    Mechanism of action
    • Stimulate β1 receptors in the heart
    • Contractility rises (positive inotrope), so stroke volume and cardiac output rise
    • Rate and conduction speed also increase
    • Selectivity differs: dobutamine is the most β1-preferring; dopamine is β1-predominant only at moderate doses and turns α1 at high doses; isoproterenol hits β1 and β2, so it also drops BP
    Therapeutic uses
    • Acute decompensated heart failure and cardiogenic shock (dobutamine — raises output with less rise in BP and oxygen demand than other catecholamines)
    • Short-term support of cardiac output after cardiac surgery
    • Cardiac stress testing when a patient cannot exercise
    • Symptomatic bradycardia and heart block unresponsive to atropine (isoproterenol)
    Side & adverse effects
    • Common: tachycardia, palpitations, headache, nausea, anxiety, mild BP change
    • Serious: dysrhythmias including ventricular ectopy, angina and myocardial ischemia from increased oxygen demand, severe hypertension or hypotension, extravasation injury
    • Isoproterenol specifically: coronary steal — blood is diverted away from injured myocardium and can enlarge an evolving MI, which is why its use has fallen
    Nursing considerations
    1Assessment
    • Correct hypovolemia first — an inotrope cannot pump a tank that is empty
    • Perfusion markers before and during: level of consciousness, capillary refill, skin temperature and colour, peripheral pulses
    • Hourly urine output — the most practical bedside sign that cardiac output improved
    • IV site every 1–2 h for blanching, coolness or swelling; phentolamine is the antidote for dopamine extravasation
    • Lung sounds and work of breathing — worsening crackles mean the heart failure is not improving
    • Chest pain — these drugs raise myocardial oxygen demand
    2Vitals
    • Continuous cardiac monitoring — watch for ectopy and new dysrhythmia, the reason to stop
    • BP continuously by arterial line, or every 5–15 min by cuff during titration
    • HR every 15 min while titrating; report a rise above 10–15% of baseline or over 100–110
    • RR and SpO2 — pulmonary congestion should ease as output improves
    • Hemodynamics where available: cardiac output, cardiac index, central venous pressure
    3Labs
    • Potassium and magnesium — β stimulation shifts K+ into cells, and low K+ or Mg2+ makes the dysrhythmias far more likely
    • BUN and creatinine for renal perfusion
    • Serum lactate — falling lactate confirms perfusion is genuinely better
    • BNP to track heart-failure status
    • Blood glucose — may rise
    • Troponin if chest pain develops
    4Patient education
    • Report chest pain, palpitations or a racing heart right away — these need assessment, not waiting
    • Tell the nurse immediately if the IV site burns, stings or swells — leaking under the skin damages tissue and is treatable if caught early
    • You will be on a heart monitor and need frequent BP checks; that is routine for this drug, not a sign something is wrong
    • Do not get out of bed without help — the drip and your blood pressure both make falls more likely
    • Expect a urinary catheter or strict intake and output; urine volume is how we know the drug is working
    • This is short-term hospital support to get the heart through a rough patch — it is not a medication you go home on
    Special populations
    • Older adults: reduced β-receptor responsiveness may blunt the effect, while coronary reserve is lower — higher risk of ischemia and dysrhythmia; monitor ECG continuously, check troponin for any chest pain, and watch renal function, since clearance is slower.
    • Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (IHSS/HOCM): contraindicated — increasing contractility worsens the left-ventricular outflow obstruction and drops cardiac output further.
    • Atrial fibrillation or flutter: dobutamine speeds AV conduction and can accelerate the ventricular response to a dangerous rate — rate control should be established first, and the rhythm watched closely.
    • Acute MI or active ischemia: increased oxygen demand can extend the infarct; isoproterenol is particularly avoided because of coronary steal.
    • Sulfite sensitivity: dobutamine and dopamine contain sodium bisulfite, which can trigger bronchospasm or anaphylaxis in sensitive asthmatic patients — ask about sulfite allergy before hanging the drip.
    • Hypovolemia: must be corrected before starting, or the drug increases oxygen demand without improving output.
    • Prolonged infusion: tolerance develops after roughly 72 hours of continuous dobutamine; a plateau in response is expected, not a pump failure.
    Beta-2 adrenergic agonists
    bronchodilatorsalbuterol · levalbuterol · salmeterol
    Example drugsalbuterol · levalbuterol · salmeterol
    Mechanism of action
    • Selectively stimulate β2 receptors on bronchial smooth muscle
    • Smooth muscle relaxes, airways widen, airflow improves
    • Also drives potassium into cells, which can lower serum potassium
    Therapeutic uses
    • Acute bronchospasm (albuterol is the rescue drug)
    • Asthma and COPD maintenance (salmeterol, long-acting)
    • Exercise-induced bronchospasm
    • Adjunct treatment of hyperkalemia
    Side & adverse effects
    • Common: tremor, jitteriness, tachycardia, palpitations, headache
    • Serious: paradoxical bronchospasm, hypokalemia, dysrhythmia, chest pain
    Nursing considerations
    1Assessment
    • Lung sounds, respiratory effort, accessory muscle use and ability to speak in full sentences — before and after every dose
    • Peak flow before and after where ordered
    • Count how often the rescue inhaler is being used — more than twice a week signals poor control
    • Check inhaler technique and whether a spacer is needed
    2Vitals
    • RR and SpO2 before and 15–20 min after; the goal is measurable improvement
    • HR before and after — expect a modest rise, report a jump above 120 or new irregularity
    • BP for a rise from β1 spillover at higher doses
    3Labs
    • Serum potassium — β2 agonists shift K+ intracellularly and can cause hypokalemia, especially with a diuretic
    • Blood glucose in diabetes — may rise
    • ABG in severe exacerbation
    4Patient education
    • Rescue vs maintenance: albuterol is for attacks; salmeterol is scheduled and will not help an attack — never substitute one for the other
    • Shakiness and a fast heartbeat are expected and usually settle
    • Rinse the mouth after inhaling
    • If you need your rescue inhaler more often than usual, call — do not just keep using it
    • Prime a new inhaler and track doses so you do not run out
    Special populations
    • Older adults: higher risk of tachydysrhythmia and tremor that interferes with inhaler handling; assess grip strength and consider a spacer or nebuliser.
    • Cardiac disease: tachycardia raises myocardial oxygen demand; monitor for angina and consider levalbuterol, which has fewer cardiac effects.
    • Diabetes: can raise blood glucose; monitor more closely during frequent dosing.
    • Pregnancy: treatment is continued — uncontrolled asthma causes fetal hypoxia, a greater risk than the drug.
    • On diuretics: additive potassium loss; check potassium before repeated dosing.
    Beta-1 selective blockers
    cardioselectivemetoprolol · atenolol · esmolol
    Example drugsmetoprolol · atenolol · esmolol
    Mechanism of action
    • Selectively block β1 receptors in heart and juxtaglomerular apparatus
    • Rate, contractility and conduction all decrease
    • Renin release falls, lowering BP
    • Selectivity is dose-dependent and lost at higher doses
    Therapeutic uses
    • Hypertension
    • Rate control in atrial fibrillation and tachyarrhythmias
    • Angina and post-MI to reduce reinfarction
    • Chronic heart failure (specific agents, titrated slowly)
    Side & adverse effects
    • Common: fatigue, dizziness, bradycardia, cold extremities
    • Serious: symptomatic bradycardia or heart block, hypotension, heart-failure exacerbation, rebound ischemia on abrupt withdrawal, masked hypoglycemia
    Nursing considerations
    1Assessment
    • Apical pulse for a full minute before each dose, against the ordered hold parameters
    • Respiratory history before the first dose
    • Dizziness, syncope, activity tolerance and peripheral perfusion
    • New oedema, weight gain or dyspnea suggesting heart failure
    2Vitals
    • HR and BP before every dose — hold and notify per the order (commonly HR < 60 or SBP < 90, but follow what is written)
    • Recheck about an hour after an oral dose for peak effect
    • Lying and standing BP if dizziness is reported
    3Labs
    • Blood glucose in diabetes — the drug masks hypoglycemic tachycardia
    • Renal function — atenolol is renally cleared and accumulates; metoprolol is hepatically metabolised
    • Electrolytes if a diuretic is co-prescribed
    • Lipid panel periodically
    4Patient education
    • Never stop abruptly — rebound chest pain, dangerous rhythms or infarction can follow; any change goes through the prescriber
    • Take metoprolol with food — it absorbs better
    • Check your pulse at home and report a rate under 60 or new dizziness
    • In diabetes, rely on sweating and hunger to detect a low — the usual racing heart may be absent
    • Rise slowly; report weight gain over 2–3 lb in a day or new ankle swelling
    Special populations
    • Older adults: increased risk of hypotension, bradycardia, dizziness and falls; start low, assess orthostatic BP, and review the med list for additive hypotensives.
    • Diabetes: blunts the tachycardia, tremor and palpitations that warn of hypoglycemia; teach sweating and hunger as the remaining cues and monitor glucose more often at initiation.
    • Asthma and COPD: preferred over nonselective agents, but selectivity is lost at higher doses — monitor for wheeze and keep a rescue inhaler available.
    • Renal impairment: atenolol accumulates and needs dose reduction; metoprolol is the safer choice.
    • Pregnancy: labetalol is preferred for chronic hypertension; atenolol is linked to fetal growth restriction and is avoided.
    Nonselective beta blockerspropranolol · carvedilol · labetalol
    Example drugspropranolol · carvedilol · labetalol
    Mechanism of action
    • Block β1 and β2 receptors
    • Cardiac effects as for β1 blockade — slower rate, lower BP
    • Loss of β2 bronchodilation allows bronchoconstriction
    • Carvedilol and labetalol also block α1, adding vasodilation
    Therapeutic uses
    • Hypertension, including hypertensive emergency (IV labetalol)
    • Angina and arrhythmias
    • Migraine prophylaxis and essential tremor (propranolol)
    • Heart failure and post-MI LV dysfunction (carvedilol)
    Side & adverse effects
    • Common: fatigue, dizziness, bradycardia, orthostatic hypotension
    • Serious: bronchospasm, heart block, acute heart-failure decompensation, masked hypoglycemia, rebound on withdrawal
    Nursing considerations
    1Assessment
    • Screen respiratory history before the first dose — the single most important assessment for this class
    • Lung sounds before and after; report new wheeze immediately
    • Apical pulse for a full minute; orthostatic BP
    • Activity tolerance and signs of fluid overload
    2Vitals
    • HR and BP before every dose against ordered parameters
    • RR and SpO2 — more important here than with selective agents because of bronchospasm risk
    • Lying and standing BP; carvedilol and labetalol add α1 blockade and orthostasis
    3Labs
    • Blood glucose in diabetes — masked hypoglycemia
    • Liver function — propranolol and carvedilol are hepatically metabolised
    • Electrolytes with concurrent diuretics
    4Patient education
    • Tell any provider you take this if you have asthma — it can trigger wheezing
    • Never stop abruptly
    • Take carvedilol with food to reduce orthostatic hypotension
    • Rise slowly and sit at the bedside before standing
    • Report new shortness of breath, wheeze, or a pulse under 60
    Special populations
    • Asthma and COPD: β2 blockade can cause severe bronchospasm — generally avoided; if unavoidable, monitor respiratory status closely and keep a rescue bronchodilator at hand.
    • Older adults: falls from orthostatic hypotension and bradycardia; assess orthostatic vitals and gait, and teach slow position changes.
    • Diabetes: masks hypoglycemia warning signs; increase glucose monitoring and teach non-adrenergic cues.
    • Heart failure: carvedilol is beneficial but must be started low and titrated slowly — a fast increase can decompensate the patient; weigh daily.
    • Pregnancy: labetalol is a preferred agent for hypertension in pregnancy.
    Alpha-1 adrenergic blockersprazosin · doxazosin · tamsulosin
    Example drugsprazosin · doxazosin · tamsulosin
    Mechanism of action
    • Block α1 receptors on vascular smooth muscle → vasodilation, BP falls
    • Block α1 in prostate and bladder neck → smooth muscle relaxes, urine flows more freely
    • Tamsulosin is relatively uroselective, so it lowers BP less
    Therapeutic uses
    • Hypertension (prazosin, doxazosin)
    • Benign prostatic hyperplasia (tamsulosin, doxazosin)
    • PTSD-related nightmares (prazosin, off-label)
    Side & adverse effects
    • Common: dizziness, orthostatic hypotension, headache, nasal congestion, fatigue
    • Serious: first-dose syncope, falls and fracture, reflex tachycardia, priapism
    Nursing considerations
    1Assessment
    • Orthostatic BP before starting and after each dose increase
    • Fall risk — gait, balance, home layout, night-time toileting
    • Urinary symptoms: stream, frequency, nocturia, sense of incomplete emptying
    • Timing — whether the patient can take the first dose safely at home
    2Vitals
    • BP lying, sitting and standing — a drop above 20 mmHg systolic on standing is significant
    • HR — reflex tachycardia may accompany the BP fall
    • Recheck 2–6 h after the first dose, when syncope risk peaks
    3Labs
    • Renal function and PSA before starting BPH therapy (the drug can alter PSA interpretation)
    • Electrolytes if on a diuretic
    • No routine drug levels needed
    4Patient education
    • Take the first dose at bedtime — it can cause fainting
    • Rise slowly; sit on the edge of the bed before standing
    • Do not drive after the first dose or a dose increase
    • Tell your eye surgeon you take tamsulosin before cataract surgery — it changes how the iris behaves during the operation
    • Report fainting, or an erection lasting over 4 hours, urgently
    Special populations
    • Older adults: markedly increased risk for orthostatic hypotension, dizziness and falls with fracture; assess orthostatic vitals, start at the lowest dose, and put fall precautions in place.
    • Cataract surgery: tamsulosin causes intraoperative floppy iris syndrome — the ophthalmologist must be told in advance, even if the drug was stopped.
    • On other antihypertensives or PDE-5 inhibitors: additive hypotension; space doses and monitor BP.
    • Women: prazosin and doxazosin are used for hypertension; tamsulosin is not indicated.
    • Syncope history: consider a supervised first dose.
    Direct-acting cholinergic agonists
    muscarinic agonistsbethanechol · pilocarpine
    Example drugsbethanechol · pilocarpine
    Mechanism of action
    • Bind muscarinic receptors directly, mimicking acetylcholine
    • Bladder detrusor contracts and the sphincter relaxes → voiding
    • GI motility and secretions increase
    • Pupils constrict; salivation increases
    Therapeutic uses
    • Nonobstructive urinary retention, including postoperative and postpartum
    • Neurogenic bladder with retention
    • Xerostomia and glaucoma (pilocarpine)
    Side & adverse effects
    • Common: salivation, sweating, abdominal cramping, urinary urgency, flushing
    • Serious: bradycardia, hypotension, bronchospasm, cholinergic crisis (SLUDGE)
    Nursing considerations
    1Assessment
    • Rule out obstruction before giving — making a blocked bladder contract can rupture it
    • Bladder scan for residual volume; palpate for distension
    • Time to first void and volume after the dose
    • Bowel sounds and abdominal comfort
    • Respiratory history — it can provoke bronchospasm
    2Vitals
    • HR and BP before and 30–60 min after — expect bradycardia and hypotension
    • RR and lung sounds for wheeze
    • Orthostatic BP before ambulating
    3Labs
    • BUN and creatinine for renal function with retention
    • Electrolytes if vomiting or diarrhea occur
    • No routine drug level
    4Patient education
    • Take on an empty stomach, an hour before or two hours after meals, to reduce nausea
    • Stay near a toilet — it works within 30–90 minutes
    • Rise slowly; it can lower blood pressure
    • Report wheezing, fainting, severe cramping or a very slow pulse
    • Do not double a missed dose
    Special populations
    • Asthma and COPD: contraindicated or high risk — bronchoconstriction and increased secretions can precipitate an attack; keep a bronchodilator available.
    • Older adults: greater risk of falls from bradycardia and orthostatic hypotension, and of urgency incontinence; ensure fast toilet access and assess orthostatic vitals.
    • Peptic ulcer disease: increased gastric acid can worsen or perforate an ulcer — contraindicated.
    • Bradycardia or heart block: further slowing is dangerous; contraindicated.
    • GI or GU obstruction: absolutely contraindicated — forcing contraction against a blockage risks rupture.
    Cholinesterase inhibitors
    indirect cholinergic agonistspyridostigmine · neostigmine · donepezil
    Example drugspyridostigmine · neostigmine · donepezil
    Mechanism of action
    • Inhibit acetylcholinesterase so ACh is not broken down
    • ACh accumulates at the synapse and its effect is prolonged
    • Improves transmission at the neuromuscular junction
    • Central agents raise ACh in the brain
    Therapeutic uses
    • Myasthenia gravis (pyridostigmine)
    • Reversal of neuromuscular blocking agents (neostigmine)
    • Alzheimer disease and Parkinson-related dementia (donepezil, rivastigmine)
    Side & adverse effects
    • Common: nausea, diarrhea, increased secretions, muscle cramps, insomnia
    • Serious: cholinergic crisis, bradycardia, bronchospasm with copious secretions, respiratory failure
    Nursing considerations
    1Assessment
    • Muscle strength, swallowing and speech, and ptosis before and after dosing
    • Respiratory effort and cough strength — weak respiratory muscles are the emergency in myasthenia
    • Distinguish cholinergic crisis (too much: SLUDGE plus weakness) from myasthenic crisis (too little: weakness without SLUDGE)
    • Baseline cognition and function for the Alzheimer agents
    2Vitals
    • HR — bradycardia is the cardiac warning sign
    • RR, SpO2 and ability to clear secretions
    • BP for hypotension
    3Labs
    • Liver function for donepezil and rivastigmine
    • Electrolytes if GI losses are significant
    • Renal function — affects clearance
    • No routine therapeutic level
    4Patient education
    • Take pyridostigmine 30–60 min before meals so swallowing is strongest when eating
    • Take the dose on time — a late dose can mean sudden weakness
    • Wear medical identification for myasthenia gravis
    • Report increasing weakness, drooling, or trouble breathing immediately — it can mean either too much or too little drug and needs assessment
    • Donepezil: give at bedtime; nausea and vivid dreams are common early and usually settle
    • Donepezil slows symptoms; it does not cure or reverse the disease
    Special populations
    • Older adults: bradycardia and syncope are more likely, and falls follow; check HR before dosing and assess for dizziness. GI effects can cause weight loss — weigh weekly.
    • Asthma and COPD: increased secretions and bronchoconstriction; monitor respiratory status closely.
    • Peptic ulcer disease: increased gastric acid may worsen ulceration.
    • Bradycardia or conduction disease: additive slowing; a pacemaker may be needed before therapy.
    • Myasthenia gravis: the crucial nursing judgement is telling cholinergic from myasthenic crisis — both present with weakness, and the treatments are opposite.
    Anticholinergics
    muscarinic antagonistsatropine · ipratropium · oxybutynin · scopolamine
    Example drugsatropine · ipratropium · oxybutynin · scopolamine
    Mechanism of action
    • Competitively block muscarinic receptors
    • Parasympathetic tone falls, leaving a sympathetic-like picture
    • Heart rate rises, secretions dry, GI and bladder smooth muscle relaxes, pupils dilate
    Therapeutic uses
    • Symptomatic bradycardia (atropine)
    • COPD maintenance bronchodilation (ipratropium, tiotropium)
    • Overactive bladder and urgency (oxybutynin)
    • Motion sickness and postoperative nausea (scopolamine)
    • Drying secretions before anaesthesia (glycopyrrolate)
    Side & adverse effects
    • Common: dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, urinary hesitancy, reduced sweating
    • Serious: urinary retention, paralytic ileus, confusion and delirium, tachydysrhythmia, hyperthermia, acute angle-closure glaucoma
    Nursing considerations
    1Assessment
    • Bladder: voiding pattern, distension, and post-void residual — retention is the effect that most often forces a stop
    • Bowel: sounds, last movement, abdominal distension
    • Mental status, especially any new confusion in an older adult
    • Vision changes and eye pain — eye pain with halos suggests acute glaucoma
    • Total anticholinergic burden across the whole medication list
    2Vitals
    • HR and rhythm — expect a rise with atropine; report a rate over 100 with symptoms
    • Temperature — reduced sweating causes hyperthermia in hot weather
    • RR and lung sounds before and after inhaled agents
    • BP for orthostatic change
    3Labs
    • Renal function — retention can cause post-renal injury; check BUN and creatinine
    • Electrolytes if there is significant constipation or reduced intake
    • No routine drug levels
    4Patient education
    • Dry mouth: sip water, use sugar-free gum or lozenges, keep up dental care — dry mouth causes cavities
    • Constipation: increase fluid and fibre, stay active; report no movement for three days
    • Report inability to urinate — this needs same-day attention
    • Avoid overheating; reduced sweating means heat is dangerous — no saunas or hard exercise in hot weather
    • Blurred vision is expected; do not drive until you know how it affects you
    • Scopolamine patch: apply behind the ear, wash hands thoroughly afterward — touching your eye causes a dilated pupil
    • Ipratropium: avoid spraying into the eyes; rinse the mouth after use
    Special populations
    • Older adults: listed on the Beers criteria as potentially inappropriate — new confusion, delirium, constipation, urinary retention or falls are often the drug rather than the disease; assess cognition at baseline and at each visit, and review total anticholinergic burden.
    • Benign prostatic hyperplasia: high risk of acute urinary retention; assess voiding and residual volume, and use with caution or avoid.
    • Narrow-angle glaucoma: pupil dilation can precipitate an acute attack — contraindicated; teach the patient to report eye pain, halos or sudden vision loss immediately.
    • Hot environments and exercise: impaired sweating causes heat intolerance and hyperthermia — a particular danger for outdoor workers, athletes and older adults in summer.
    • Children: more susceptible to hyperthermia and CNS effects; monitor temperature and behaviour closely.

    Why it is built by class, not by drug. Karch teaches prototypes — the model drug its class is compared against. Learn epinephrine and you can reason about any adrenergic agonist; learn atropine and every anticholinergic follows the same dry–dilated–retained–confused pattern.

    Self-test

    QuestionAnswer
    An IM injection is given to a 35-year-old male (165 lb) and an 18-year-old female (125 lb). Who absorbs the drug faster, and why?The male. He has more vascular muscle mass, and absorption from an IM site depends on blood flow to that muscle. The female has a higher body-fat percentage and less vascular muscle, so absorption is slower. (Lecture slides 15–16.)
    True or false: poisoning occurs when too much of a drug causes damage to multiple body systems.True. An overdose produces multi-system damage and requires urgent supportive care plus antidote therapy where one exists. (Lecture slides 24–25.)
    A patient with low serum albumin is given a normal dose of a highly protein-bound drug. What happens?More drug remains unbound (free) and pharmacologically active, so a normal dose can produce toxicity. Only free drug exerts an effect.
    Why can the oral dose of a drug be much larger than its IV dose?The first-pass effect. Orally absorbed drug passes through the liver via the portal vein and is partly metabolized before reaching systemic circulation. IV bypasses this entirely.
    A patient develops a rash on the first-ever dose of a drug. Is this most likely an allergy?Unlikely as a true allergy — hypersensitivity requires prior sensitizing exposure. Consider an idiosyncratic or secondary reaction. Still stop the drug, notify, and document.
    Which two organ systems, when diseased, most change how a drug behaves — and which ADME stage does each affect?Liver (metabolism) and kidneys (excretion). Impairment of either causes the drug to accumulate toward toxic levels at ordinary doses.
    Week 2 warm-up. Order: metoprolol 25 mg PO now. MAR: hold if systolic BP < 100. Current BP 88/54. Give it, hold and assess, or ask the patient?Hold and assess / clarify. The dose is correct, but a hold parameter is met. The “right dose” is not enough — assessment and clinical judgment come first. Asking the patient what they prefer does not resolve a safety parameter.
    Mini-case A. Patient says penicillin caused hives. New order: amoxicillin.Stop and clarify before giving. Hives is a true allergic reaction, and amoxicillin is a penicillin-class antibiotic — cross-reactivity is the concern. Verify the reaction, notify the prescriber.
    Mini-case B. Order: acetaminophen 650 mg. Available: 325 mg tablets.Proceed — this is a normal calculation. 650 ÷ 325 × 1 = 2 tablets. Two tablets is a reasonable answer for a dose twice the tablet strength.
    Mini-case C. Order to give now. Patient asks “what is this for?”Stop and teach before giving. Answer the question — name and purpose. An informed patient is the last safety check, and consent depends on it.
    Mini-case D. Order reads 0.5 mg. The MAR displays .5 mg.Clarify. A naked decimal is an ISMP error-prone designation — .5 mg can be read as 5 mg. Confirm the intended dose and report the display as a hazardous condition.
    Integrated case. Ms. Alvarez, 67, discharged yesterday, says she takes half of some pills when money is tight, uses an unlisted OTC sleep aid and herbal supplement, saw a post claiming supplements are safer than prescriptions, and asks what her new pain medicine is for. Order: acetaminophen 650 mg; available 325 mg tablets.Assess: full medication history including the unlisted OTC and herbal, last dose, allergies, pain, liver history (acetaminophen), and her understanding.
    Barriers: cost-driven dose-splitting, polypharmacy with undisclosed products, information quality from social media, a care transition, and a health-literacy gap.
    Teach: name and purpose, the acetaminophen ceiling and its presence in combination OTC products, why the supplement must be disclosed, and cost options — then teach-back.
    Math: 650 ÷ 325 × 1 = 2 tablets, if it is otherwise safe to give.
    Her four layers of a safe medication plan.1. Right patient and assessment — is this appropriate now? 2. Right medication plan — order, dose, route, timing, indication, monitoring. 3. Right calculation — units match, correct math, reasonable answer. 4. Right real-world plan — can the patient understand, obtain, and safely continue it?
    Medication safety = clinical judgment + calculation + communication + systems thinking.
